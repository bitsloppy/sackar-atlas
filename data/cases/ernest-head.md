## Who was Ernest Head

Ernest Allan Head was 44 years old when he was killed in his apartment in Summer Hill on the evening of 17 June 1976. Born in Malaysia in 1932, he had emigrated to Australia in 1960. He had lived in Australia for sixteen years at the time of his death.

Friends and colleagues described him as a quiet, private, and good-natured man who lived alone and worked as a clerk with the Corporate Affairs Commission. He was, by all accounts, immaculate in his personal appearance — "soft hearted, kind, shy," said one colleague; "always immaculate, shirts pressed, not a hair out of place, always spick and span," said a friend.

Ernest Head was a gay man. He had previously been in a three-year relationship with a man known to the Inquiry as I454, with whom he lived. He had disclosed his sexuality to his brother, Arthur Head. A diary found in his flat after his death contained contact details for a number of men with whom he had had previous sexual contact. He appears not to have been out in all contexts — a workplace friend said she was unaware of his sexuality — which may reflect the substantial prejudice against LGBTIQ people in 1976, before decriminalisation.

Head had been experiencing financial difficulties at the time of his death, but there is no evidence his death was connected to his financial situation.

## The night of 17 June 1976

Ernest Head was last seen alive at the Bank Hotel, Newtown, on the evening of 17 June 1976. He had met his friend I454 there for a drink from 5:00pm; I454 left at around 6:05pm, and Head remained.

Head is believed to have attended a nearby TAB shortly afterwards. He was never seen alive again.

At around 7:00pm, neighbours in the adjacent unit heard a disturbance: a male voice they identified as Ernest Head calling out "Help me," "Don't hurt me," "Please don't hurt me." A man in the neighbouring flat knocked on Head's door; Head responded through the door that "everything's alright." The neighbour returned to his own flat. About an hour later, neighbours heard two men's voices, and what appeared to be a normal conversation — then nothing further.

Ernest Head's body was found at around 11:55pm on 22 June 1976, five days after he died, when I454 and another friend came to enquire after his welfare. His body was naked and lying face down in the kitchen, covered in blood. He had been stabbed 35 times to the face, chest, abdomen, back, buttocks and limbs.

There was no sign of forced entry. Various items of property were stolen. Blood was found throughout the flat — kitchen, lounge room, dining room, hallway, and main bedroom — suggesting a sustained and violent attack that moved through the apartment. On the kitchen wall above the body were marks in blood resembling handprints.

## The police investigation

The original investigation by Ashfield Police Station, assisted by the Special Crime Squad, was extensive and thorough — commended by Sackar as one of the better original investigations among the cases the Inquiry examined. Police interviewed over a hundred people, canvassed bars and hotels, sought Interpol assistance to contact family in Malaysia, issued a $10,000 reward, and traced the names in Head's diary and wallet. Despite the scope of the work, no suspects emerged who were not ultimately excluded.

However, two detectives referred to Head's likely sexual activity prior to his death as consistent with "what we have been told about the nefarious activities of the deceased" — an indicator of negative police attitudes toward gay men in 1976, before decriminalisation. Sackar accepted that this language did not impact the quality of the investigation.

The subsequent management of physical exhibits was far less adequate. By 2005, of over 60 items originally seized — including semen-stained materials, blood samples, and the knife — only three cigarette butts and a match remained. This loss was found by Sackar to be "not consistent with proper police practice." Had Engin Simsek been alive and prosecuted, the loss of exhibits might have seriously impaired any prosecution.

## Engin Simsek — a palm print identified 47 years later

In May 2023, at the direction of the Sackar Inquiry, FASS re-examined the palm prints from the crime scene using V11 NAFIS technology. A palm print in blood — found on the kitchen wall above Ernest Head's body — was matched to Engin Simsek.

Simsek was born in Türkiye on 3 September 1943 and had emigrated to Australia on 31 May 1972. His relatives had lived on Grosvenor Crescent — the same street as Ernest Head — and his arrival card recorded his intended address as Grosvenor Crescent. Simsek's own record fingerprints had been in police files since his arrest in 1980 for a malicious injury offence; however, earlier palm print matching technology was insufficient to identify the match. The V11 matcher upgrade in 2015 ultimately enabled the identification.

Sackar could not make a positive finding as to the role Simsek played in Head's death — it is possible he was not alone, and the precise circumstances remain unknown. What is clear is that his palm print, in blood, on the wall directly above Head's body, places him at or near the scene at or around the time of death.

Simsek departed Australia on 13 August 1994. He died by suicide in Türkiye on 6 May 1999. No criminal proceedings are now possible.

## What Sackar found

Sackar upheld the finding of City Coroner Nash:

> Ernest Head died on or about 17 June 1976 in Summer Hill from wilfully inflicted stab wounds of the chest and abdomen.

He found that, on the available evidence, there is "objectively reason to suspect that LGBTIQ bias was a factor in Mr Head's death." He noted the possibility that the attack was committed in response to, or as a result of, sexual activity that had just taken place — and that such an attack, driven by disgust or anger at having participated in intercourse with a man, would constitute an LGBTIQ-motivated killing. The theft of property did not exclude this possibility.

Sackar made no specific recommendations in relation to Ernest Head's death, noting that global recommendations on exhibit retention and preservation are addressed in Chapter 8.
